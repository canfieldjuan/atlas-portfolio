# Body Copy Outline — Alternative (Question-Led)

Same arguments, same data, same claim boundaries as the primary outline.
Different presentation: each section opens with the question the reader is
silently asking at that moment. This creates a conversational reading
experience — the page feels like it's answering, not pitching.

---

## SECTION 1 — "What's this actually costing me?"

**Landing-page zone:** Below the hero. Opens with the reader's own question.

**Structure:** Lead with the hard number. No setup paragraph. The question is
the section header — the body answers it directly.

> **What's this actually costing me?**

Gartner benchmarked it. A self-service resolution costs $1.84. A human-handled
contact costs $13.50. That's a 7.3x multiplier on every ticket that should
never have reached an agent.

HDI's 2024 Support Center Practices report puts the gap even wider: $22.00 for
a live agent interaction versus $2.93 for self-service.

Across the full resolution journey — customer searches, fails, switches
channels, repeats information — Gartner found the gap reaches 80 to 100 times.

And 40 to 60% of your inbound volume is repetitive. Password resets. "Where's
my order." "How do I cancel." "Why was I charged." Questions your help center
was supposed to answer.

Your agents are spending 5 hours a week on copy-paste replies. NICE's research
on contact center burnout describes the daily reality in their own words:
"Answering the same question over and over again isn't very rewarding." "A day
in the life of an agent is filled with mind-numbing repetition."

That repetition has a cost beyond the hourly wage. It's the leading driver of
agent turnover — 31 to 45% annually. Each departure costs $10,000 to $20,000
to replace. 74% of agents report ongoing burnout. 90% of IT support
professionals say repetitive manual tasks actively damage team morale.

And on the customer side: Gartner found that 73% of customers try self-service
first. Only 14% fully resolve there. The other 59% arrive at your queue already
frustrated — and 74% of customers say having to repeat information is what
frustrates them most.

The CEB research behind The Effortless Experience quantified what that
frustration costs: 94% of customers with low-effort experiences intend to
repurchase. Only 4% of high-effort customers say the same. 81% intend to spread
negative word of mouth.

You're not just paying for tickets. You're paying for churn.

## SECTION 2 — "Why haven't I fixed this already?"

**Landing-page zone:** The pivot. Acknowledges the effort they've already made.

**Structure:** Name the 6 things they've tried. Validate each. Then show the
pattern — every attempt failed for the same reason: no closed loop between
what customers ask and what the help center says.

> **Why haven't I fixed this already?**

You've tried.

You hired more agents. But at 30 to 45% annual turnover and $10,000 to $20,000
per replacement, linear hiring is a leaky bucket. The math doesn't close.

You built out the knowledge base. It degraded within six months. Content went
stale. Search stayed poor. Nobody owned the feedback loop from ticket to
article gap. Gartner found that 43% of self-service failures happen because
customers can't find content — even when it exists.

You deployed a chatbot. Klarna's CEO said publicly in May 2025 what a lot of
teams discovered quietly: "As cost unfortunately seems to have been a too
predominant evaluation factor, what you end up having is lower quality."
Intercom Fin users report real-world resolution rates far below the vendor
case studies — often because conversations get marked "assumed resolved" the
moment a human steps in.

You wrote macros. They went outdated. Became the reason the bot sent the wrong
article. Gorgias's own product copy admits: "Generic, robotic responses
frustrate customers."

You might have tried outsourcing. First-year attrition runs higher offshore.
Quality drops on complex tickets. Klarna tried BPO, pivoted to AI, and then
walked AI back — the full loop — because none of it addressed the root cause.

None of these failed because you did them wrong. They failed because none of
them close the loop between what a customer asks and what your help center
says. The answer gets written — in a ticket reply, in a macro, in a KB article
nobody maintains — and then it disappears.

**Landing-page zone:** The mechanism reveal.

**Structure:** Three numbered steps. No preamble — the question opens the
section. The answer is the mechanism.

  search and what your help center says.

**Step 3: You review and publish. Nothing goes live without you.**
You read each recommendation. You edit each FAQ. You decide what ships. Pages
go up using the exact language your customers already type into Google. Google
indexes them. The next customer who searches that question finds your page —
not your support form.

**Why this works beyond the help center:**
Pages written in the language people actually search get more visibility on
Google. This is the foundational principle of SEO — not a theory, not a case
study, demonstrable by any buyer in thirty seconds with their own Google
search. Your ticket queue already contains that language. Customers type "how
do I cancel my account." Your help center says "Subscription Termination
Procedure." Google can't match them. That gap is a ticket. Close it, and
Google starts deflecting.

**What this does NOT do:**
- Does not answer tickets.
- Does not touch your help desk.
- Does not publish anything without your review.
- Does not replace agents.
- Does not require a consultant or a six-month rollout.

## SECTION 4 — "How do I know this is real?"

**Landing-page zone:** The proof section.

**Structure:** No preamble. Four proof blocks, from strongest (product-level)
to borrowed (industry data) to third-party authority.

> **How do I know this is real?**

**Every recommendation links to a real ticket.**
Not a model's best guess. Not synthesized from training data. For every finding
in your ranked fix list, you can click through to the source — the exact
ticket, the verbatim customer language, the precise moment the gap occurred.
You can verify every claim yourself.

**The queue sets the priority — not opinions.**
The fix list is ordered by how often each question reaches support. Not by
which agent complained loudest. Not by which topic the team assumes is the
biggest problem. The data decides. And because frequency correlates with search
demand, the highest-ranked fixes are also the highest-impact SEO opportunities.

**FAQs are drafted from real answers that already worked.**
Every draft FAQ is assembled from the actual responses your support team wrote
across closed tickets — in the language that already resolved the issue for a
real customer. You're not inventing answers. You're publishing the answers your
team already proved.

**The industry data backs the problem — and the mechanism.**
Gartner benchmarked the cost gap: $1.84 self-service vs $13.50 assisted.
Gartner measured the resolution rate: only 14% of issues fully resolve in
self-service, despite 73% of customers attempting it. SQM Group found that a
1% improvement in First Contact Resolution reduces operating costs by 1%. CEB
research across 97,000 customers found that 94% of low-effort customers intend
to repurchase — versus 4% for high-effort.

Eric Keller, Senior Director at Gartner: "While 73% of customers use
self-service at some point in their customer service journey, it's concerning
to see that so few fully resolve there."

Nick Toman, co-author of The Effortless Experience: "We count that as yet
another 'first contact resolved,' but the customer views it as 'I had to
contact you twice.'"

**The Klarna lesson: deflection without quality is a different cost line.**
In February 2024, Klarna's AI handled 2.3 million conversations in a month —
"the equivalent work of 700 full-time agents." By May 2025, the CEO walked it
back publicly: "As cost unfortunately seems to have been a too predominant
evaluation factor, what you end up having is lower quality." The lesson isn't
that automation doesn't work. It's that automation without human review
produces automated mistakes. Support Ticket Deflection is built on that lesson:
nothing goes live without you.

## HOW THIS DIFFERS FROM THE PRIMARY OUTLINE

| Element | Primary outline | This outline |
|---|---|---|
| Section headers | Descriptive ("Problem Agitation") | The reader's question ("What's this actually costing me?") |
| Section 2 | Current Way vs This Way (comparison table) | "Why haven't I fixed this already?" (validation + acknowledgment of effort) |
| Opening | Lead with the number | Lead with the question, then the number |
| Objection handling | Separate section (§4 in primary) | Woven into §2 ("Why haven't I fixed this already?") and §6 ("What if it doesn't work?") |
| Tone | Journalistic / report-like | Conversational — the page answers what the reader is thinking |
| Reader posture | "I'm reading an argument" | "This page is answering my questions" |

## WHEN TO USE THIS VERSION

Best for: low-friction conversion paths (the reader already knows they have
a problem and is evaluating solutions), A/B tests against the primary outline,
and pages where the headline has already done the heavy lifting of naming
the problem.

Not ideal for: cold traffic that needs the problem established fully before
the mechanism reveal. Use the primary outline there.
